The Forward

A 529 plan is not just for college saving.

Plans began as college savings vehicles. Three decades of legislation have made them K–12 accounts, post-secondary credentialing accounts, retirement on-ramps, and estate-planning instruments. You cannot "just get your money back if it isn't used for college" — because the money never left you. It remained yours, and you remain the owner until you decide to transfer that ownership away.

When you die, a 529 requires no probate; it is not counted as part of your estate. Thirty-five percent of families use a college savings fund. Fifty-four percent of parents say they do not know enough about 529 plans to enroll. This is an attempt to close that gap.

C. Richard Hopkins, MD, CRPC · 11 chapters · 150 pages · 97 worked examples

Six things to know first

  1. You are the owner. The funds can be returned to you at any time.
  2. K–12 tuition, materials, and tutoring now qualify — up to $20,000/yr.
  3. Leftover funds roll to the beneficiary's Roth IRA, up to $35,000.
  4. Up to $10,000 lifetime may repay the beneficiary's student loans, and $10,000 a sibling's.
  5. A couple may move $190,000 per beneficiary out of a taxable estate at once.
  6. Beneficiaries may be changed to any family member — including yourself — free.

Chapter IV · Using the 529 for K–12  ·  Rule 4.3

You may pay for a tutor through a 529 account.

Federal · effective 2026 · limited IRS guidance
The federal rule

Qualified expenses include tuition for tutoring or educational classes taken outside the home — including at a tutoring facility — if the tutor or instructor is not related to the student and is (a) licensed as a teacher in any state, (b) has taught at an eligible educational institution, or (c) is a subject-matter expert in the relevant subject.

A math, English, or second-language Spanish after-school tutor working outside your home should qualify. A violin teacher functions similarly to a tutor: highly personalized, one-on-one instruction focused on individual progress rather than group teaching. A dance instructor leading large studio classes is more likely an instructor than a tutor — the distinction turns on setting and class size.

Example · Paul

Paul, a former spelling-bee champion, pays for private spelling lessons for his four grandchildren at an expert's home. The unrelated teacher signs a note confirming subject-matter expertise and individual instruction. Paul keeps records in case of an audit or a change in IRS guidance.

Gray area — proceed carefully There is currently no IRS case guidance on what constitutes a "tutor," an "educational class," or a "subject-matter expert," and no announced timeline for it. Live by the letter of the law:
  • Keep every receipt.
  • Have instructors sign that they are licensed, have taught at an eligible institution, or are subject-matter experts.
  • Confirm they are unrelated to the student and instruct outside your home.
  • Be willing to change course as guidance arrives.

Thirty years

Full history →
1996Section 529 created. The Small Business Job Protection Act authorizes Qualified Tuition Programs. Utah launches the same year.
2001Withdrawals become tax-free. EGTRRA exempts qualified distributions from federal income tax.
2006Made permanent. The Pension Protection Act removes EGTRRA's sunset.
2012Technology qualifies. ATRA adds computers, software, and internet access.
2018K–12 tuition qualifies. TCJA adds $10,000/yr; 37 states and D.C. conform.
2019Apprenticeships and loans. The SECURE Act adds both, with a $10,000 lifetime loan cap.
2024The Roth rollover. SECURE 2.0 permits up to $35,000 into the beneficiary's Roth IRA.
2025Tutors and credentials. H.R. 1 broadens K–12 expenses and vocational credentialing; ABLE rollovers made permanent.

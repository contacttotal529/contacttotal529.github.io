Myth

"It's only for college."

It's a K–12 account, a trade-school account, a credentialing account, and an estate-planning account too. Up to $20,000 a year can go toward K–12 tuition, curriculum, and tutoring.

Myth

"I lose the money if they skip college."

The money never left you. You're the owner until you decide otherwise — and you can change the beneficiary to a sibling, a cousin, or yourself, free, at any time.

Myth

"Leftover money is wasted."

Up to $35,000 rolls into the beneficiary's Roth IRA. Another $10,000 can pay down their student loans — and $10,000 more for a sibling's.

Real situations

Nearly a hundred worked examples

Every rule comes with a family. Browse by who you are.

Student · Financial aid
Vinni

Vinni saved $10,000 himself for college — and discovered student assets are assessed at 20%, cutting his aid by $2,000. His grandparents open a 529 in his name and hold the money instead. Grandparent-owned accounts are assessed at 0%.

Parent · Beneficiary change
Bob

Bob goes back for a night-school MBA at 40. He owns 529s for his daughters, ages 6 and 4, and they won't need them for years — so he names himself beneficiary, finishes the degree, and switches them back two years later.

Grandparent · Control
Isaac

Isaac wants to fund his granddaughter's education but worries his son would misuse the account. He names his trust as successor owner, so a responsible trustee — not the son — takes over if Isaac dies.

Parent · Employer
Don

Don wants the best for his 15 employees. As CEO he opens institutional accounts contributing up to $1,000 per employee per year, split between their own student debt or continuing education and their children's accounts.

Grandparent · Estate
Robert

Robert inherits $500,000 at 69 and doesn't need it. He gifts $19,000 into a 529 for each of his 6 children, their spouses, and 10 grandchildren — 22 accounts — and collects a state tax benefit on the way out.

1996 → 2026

Thirty years of getting better

The account your parents opened for tuition is not the account you have today. Congress has expanded it roughly every four years — and almost nobody has been told.

Read the full history →
1996

Section 529 is born

The Small Business Job Protection Act gives states authority to run Qualified Tuition Programs. Utah launches the same year.

2001

Withdrawals become tax-free

EGTRRA exempts qualified distributions from federal income tax — the change that made 529s genuinely powerful.

2018

K–12 tuition qualifies

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act adds up to $10,000 a year for elementary and secondary tuition. 37 states conform.

2019

Apprenticeships and student loans

The SECURE Act adds registered apprenticeships, plus $10,000 lifetime toward the beneficiary's student debt.

2024

The Roth rollover arrives

SECURE 2.0 lets unused funds move into the beneficiary's Roth IRA — up to $35,000, with no income limit.

2025–26

Tutors, credentials, and ABLE

H.R. 1 broadens K–12 to tutoring and materials, extends eligibility to vocational credentials, and makes ABLE rollovers permanent.
